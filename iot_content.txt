Refik Can OZTAS1[n23131610]

IoT devices are starting to be used more day by day. Although these
devices are very useful even with their limited resources, their security is a
great attack vector. This paper covers and investigates several attacks on
real-life created IoT networks via hardwares to conduct those attacks.
Also, as the created network topology, future work directions are also
included in this paper.
Keywords: IoT Security, Wireless Security, ZigBee Security, Wi-Fi Security.
1
Introduction
The need to secure the Internet of Things (IoT) is more important than ever
as the globe grows more interconnected due to the vast network of smart gadgets. IoT
technologies are expanding quickly, which presents a number of opportunities but also
raises a serious concern: these linked ecosystems are vulnerable to malevolent threats.
This case study explores the obstacles, tactics, and innovations in protecting the
confidentiality and integrity of connected devices, delving into the dynamic field of
IoT security.
In the age of digital transformation, where IoT interlaces homes, industries,
healthcare, and transportation seamlessly, the imperative for steadfast security
measures cannot be overstated. My research seeks to untangle the intricacies of IoT
security, confronting the distinctive challenges stemming from the diversity of
devices, protocols, and communication channels inherent in the IoT terrain.
Notable cases, such as the Mirai botnet attack and the Dyn DDoS attack in
2016, demonstrated the potential havoc that can be wreaked when insecure IoT
devices are exploited to orchestrate large-scale disruptions. These incidents exposed
critical security flaws in widely used IoT devices, illustrating the urgent need for
robust cybersecurity measures.
Although IoT security covers many security areas like Network Security,
Hardware Security and many more, and there are other case studies that cover [15]
every area, this case study mostly focuses on the Wireless Security and Network
Security side of IoT devices.

Methodology
2.1 Security of IoT devices
A physical object or device that has sensors, software, and connection built
in to enable it to communicate and share data with other devices and systems via the
internet is referred to as an Internet of Things (IoT) device. These gadgets are a
component of a larger ecosystem called the Internet of Things, in which devices may
gather, send, and receive data for a variety of uses thanks to their connections and
interactions with one another.
IoT devices contain various components. For an attacker, every component
of this device can be an attack surface. Basic logical and physical components of an
IoT device are:
●
Network
Since the devices are connected with each other and gateways/routers. Their
network connection is a security concern.
●
Web (Front & Backend and Web services)
Most end-user type IoT devices have web panels in local network and cloud
networks for usability. These panels can be important attack vectors.
●
Mobile App (Android & iOS applications)
Most end-user type IoT devices have mobile applications for usability. These
applications can be important attack vectors.
●
Wireless Connectivity (Zigbee, WiFi, Bluetooth, etc)
The emergence of wireless networking technologies, including WiFi,
Bluetooth, Zigbee, and others, has greatly expanded the potential of Internet of Things

3.1 ZigBee Channel Fuzzing/Scanning
ZigBee uses 2.4GHz channels. Based on the RFC, it starts from 2405MHz
(0x0B channel) to 2480MHz (0x1A channel). Although packets can be sent via
different channels with-in the same network, most cases use one frequency channel.
Listening every channel with time-slots, called Channel Fuzzing/Scanning, is a
reconnaissance step of ZigBee attacks.
I listened to all channels with 1-second time slots. In my case channel 0x0E,
2420MHz was used by ZigBee gateway and sensors.

3.2 Network Sniffing
ZigBee protocol sends packets encrypted, however, this encryption can be
decrypted easily. [11]. When hardware sets properly, it can act as sender and receiver
in ZigBee protocol. [Appendix A]. When traffic is captured, I see encrypted traffic.
ZigBee uses two keys because of its protocol. Trust Center Link Key and Network
Key. Trust Center key is a generic key in ZigBee protocol. Although some specific
vendors like Hue [12], use different trust center keys, it was generic in my case.
Detailed instructions are shown in Appendix B [Appendix B].
Network is a secret component, however when a new device is joined to the
network, the ZigBee Gateway must send this key to the device in order to
communicate. As an attacker, I can sniff this key.
When a device wants to join the network, it sends a broadcast request. When
gateway captures these requests, it responds with Permit Join Request, which includes
a network key. With the capture key, the attacker can read frames.

3.2 Packet Injection/Manipulation
Since a packet can be unencrypted and readable, it can be manipulated also.
For instance, in our network, sensors send temperature data to ZigBee Gateway.
Although jamming a specific packet and forging a manipulated one is not
easily-implementable, sending newly crafted packets can manipulate Gateway,
ultimately the whole architecture. For instance, if there is a rule that enables a
cooling-system in a specific temperature, it can be easily triggered.

3.4 Wi-Fi Protocol of ZigBee Gateway
WiFi deauthentication (deauth) attack is a type of cyber attack that targets
wireless networks. The goal of this attack is to disconnect devices from a WiFi
network by sending deauthentication frames to the targeted devices. These frames
essentially tell the devices that they are no longer authenticated to the network,
forcing them to disconnect and prompting them to reauthenticate.

Related Works
In general, wireless attacks may be classified into three basic section:
Packet sniffing
Packet injection
Packet manipulation
Sniffing
can
be
considered passive reconnaissance in the offensive-security
methodology. Only confidentiality of the data is an attack surface. An attacker can
sniff packets without generating any logs.
Injection and manipulation can be considered more complex attacks. It aims at
Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability of data.
Although not every ZigBee attack is directly related to ZigBee protocol, it can be
caused by another layer protocols, based on the paper by M. Gupta, S. Singh [3],
general ZigBee Weaknesses and Suggestions [2], Routing Layer Intrusions [4],
ZigBee Vulnerabilities [5] , Zigbee Topology [6] , Zigbee Concepts [7], General
Overview [8], Key Management [9], Zigbee and IEEE 802.15.4 [10], Zigbee Attack
Classification [3], are the mostly studied areas in the domain of ZigBee.

5
Future Work Direction
My case study required comparison and merging of various attacks on the
Wireless Security side of IoT devices. Based on this general study, future direction on
this research might be:
●
Since there is a PoC network and architecture for ZigBee, a dataset can be
created from real ZigBee traffic.
●
Based on this traffic, implemented attacks can be generated in the traffic.
And, study for solving anomaly detection problems, classification of attack
problems can be conducted via Machine Learning / Artificial Intelligence /
Neural Network based approaches.
●
Although this paper covers mostly wireless attacks of IoT devices, other
security components might be studied as a case study.
●
Based on the traffic, IPS/IDS systems can be formed.
●
With a proper IDS/IPS system, if hardware of devices is also studied, there
can be Endpoint Based Security Solutions for such systems.
●
In addition, as a deep-dive security approach, vulnerabilities on ZigBee
protocol, or physically attack vectors (such as jamming with SDR) could be
studied and analyzed.
6
Conclusion
My research is a basic security case study on IoT devices. This research
starts with definitions and IoT devices security of components. Although this paper
mostly covers the wireless security of IoT devices, other components security is also
mentioned. In order to conduct a case study, I created an IoT network with various
devices, made the configuration and set-up the hardware devices in order to conduct
ZigBee attacks.
As mentioned in future directions, this case study may be extended to
generate dataset, characterize dataset and furthermore. My generated ZigBee data (not
all) is referenced in Appendix E [Appendix E].
Although ZigBee is a cheap technology for low-resource devices, its security
in IoT seems problematic. Hong Kong CERT team published a security guideline [13]
to prevent attacks just like in this case study, however, since the area like IoT is very
vendor dependent, forming and conducting security principles may not be possible in
near future.

APPENDIX A
Hardware Setup
I used a CC2531 USB Dongle in order to capture and send packets. Although
its hardware is specialized for ZigBee, in order to work with real-life cases it needs
firmware. Firmwares of CC2531 is directly related to which packet capture
technology you are using. Therefore, in the research process, I tried various packet
capture softwares and firmwares.
In order to install firmware, CC Debugger by Texas Instruments are required.
In addition, debugger has 10 pins and the CC2531 has 8, therefore a transformer cable
is also required.
After that, with Texas Instruments software, firmware can be uploaded to
SoC, and related packet sniffers can be used.
In this paper, if Ubiqua protocol analyzer is used in the attack, CC2531 uses
ubqia-sn_v0.9.0.hex firmware. If wireshark is used, it uses zboss_sniffer.hex
firmware.
